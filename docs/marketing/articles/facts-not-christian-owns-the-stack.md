# They Aren’t Even a Christian Organization — and They Still Own the School Software

**Subtitle:** FACTS is Nelnet (NYSE: NNI). Selling into faith-based schools is a market. It is not a ministry.

**Status:** Draft for boards, principals, and parent leaders · Beacon / Common Cents IP  
**Related live page:** [Beacon vs FACTS](https://beacon.commoncentsip.com/vs/facts) (when PR #17 is merged)

---

Walk into almost any Christian school office in America and you will find the same stack humming in the background.

Tuition plans. Financial aid worksheets. Gradebook. Attendance. Report cards. Parent portal. Family app. Payment reminders. Sometimes even the lunch line.

The logo on the login screen often says **FACTS**.

The assumption in the hallway is quiet and almost never challenged: *they must be “our people.” They work with Christian schools. The name sounds solid. The sales rep knows our calendar. Surely this is ministry-adjacent software.*

Here is the blunt version.

**FACTS is not a Christian organization.**  
It is commercial education software under **Nelnet, Inc.** — a publicly traded company (**NYSE: NNI**). Selling hard into Catholic, Protestant, and independent faith-based schools is a **customer segment**, not a calling.

Faith *market* is not faith *identity*.

That distinction matters more than most boards admit before they sign another three-year renewal.

---

## How we got a Wall Street ticker in the sanctuary hallway

FACTS did not appear overnight as a “Christian SIS.”

- **1986** — FACTS Management starts as tuition payment plans for private and parochial schools.
- **2005–2006** — **Nelnet** acquires FACTS (majority stake, then the rest) and folds it into a business-solutions / education-services portfolio.
- **2014** — FACTS acquires **RenWeb**, one of the dominant student information systems for private and faith-based K–12 schools.

If your school still googles “RenWeb alternative,” you are usually already on the FACTS SIS product line — same corporate umbrella, same suite gravity, same family fatigue with a different memory of the brand.

None of that is illegal. None of that requires a conspiracy. Public companies buy distribution. Private schools need tuition rails and a place to put grades. The market does what markets do.

The problem is spiritual and operational at the same time: **Christian schools keep mistaking vendor presence in the faith market for shared mission.**

## “But they have *everything*”

Yes. That is the point — and the trap.

Suite vendors win by becoming the plumbing:

| Layer | What schools feel |
|-------|-------------------|
| Tuition / payment plans | Hard to leave — money is scary |
| Aid / needs assessment | Deep workflows schools do not want to rebuild |
| SIS (grades, attendance, roster) | Daily teacher muscle memory |
| Family portal / Family App | “Parents already have an account” |
| Messaging / alerts | Assumed to be “handled” |

Once tuition, SIS, and the family app sit under one vendor, leaving feels like moving the building. That is **lock-in with a smile**. Sales calls it integration. Boards call it “we’re already all-in.”

And because the brand shows up at every Christian-school conference, the suite starts to feel inevitable — as if Kingdom work *requires* Nelnet’s stack.

It does not.

## What “taking advantage” looks like without a villain speech

You do not need to invent malice. You only need mismatched incentives.

**Public company software** optimizes for scale, retention, and national product roadmaps.  
**Christian schools** optimize for discipleship, academics, trust with families, and stewardship of tuition dollars.

When those diverge, the school usually loses quietly:

- Parents open the portal less every year.
- The office cannot prove a message landed — or that a parent reply became a school record.
- Teachers invent GroupMe and text threads leadership cannot see.
- Peak-season support is built for a national queue, not your 110 families and a principal who also teaches a class.
- Renewal season arrives with spiritual language in the pitch and commercial terms in the PDF.

Corporate America does not have to hate the Church to extract value from it. It only has to treat Christian schools as a **vertical**.

## Is FACTS a Christian organization? Short answer: no.

Parents and boards ask this constantly. Give them a clean answer.

- **Not** a church  
- **Not** a denomination  
- **Not** a Christian ministry  
- **Yes** a Nelnet commercial brand that sells into faith-based schools  

Marketing to Christians is not the same as being a Christian organization. A logo wall of Christian schools is a customer list. It is not a statement of shared accountability before God.

If your board evaluates vendors with spiritual language, evaluate them on **spiritual outcomes you can measure**: honesty, care for families, transparency about what is live vs log-only, and whether tuition dollars fund ministry or a ticker.

## A sane next step (that is not rip-and-replace theater)

Rip-and-replace is how suites win the argument.

Stewardship often looks like a **phased pilot**:

1. Keep FACTS tuition for a season if that is what cash-flow reality requires.  
2. Move the family layer — grades parents open, digests, logged messages — onto something calm.  
3. Renegotiate the money rails when the board has bandwidth and trust data.

You do not owe Nelnet your entire school operating system because they once bought RenWeb and put “faith-based” on a slide.

---

## For the board packet

Ask these before the next renewal:

1. Is this vendor a ministry — or a public company selling into ministries?  
2. Can our office prove parent messages delivered *and* replies recorded?  
3. Do families actually open what we send — or only complain in the car line?  
4. What would it cost (in trust, not just dollars) to keep tuition and leave the family portal?  
5. Who benefits when “everything is connected” — our 110 kids, or the suite’s retention metrics?

## Closing

FACTS may not be a Christian organization at all — and yet, for thousands of schools, they still hold the software stack that shapes how parents hear from teachers, how tuition feels, and how “communication” gets defined.

That is not inevitable. It is a habit.

Christian schools can keep buying scale. Or they can start buying **stewardship** — software that tells the truth, treats families like people, and does not confuse a New York Stock Exchange ticker with a mission partner.

**That’s the FACTS.**
